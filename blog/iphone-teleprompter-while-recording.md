The iPhone's built-in Camera app has no teleprompter, so to read a script while you record you need either a camera app with a teleprompter on the recording screen, such as ContentCam (which I build; the teleprompter is a Premium feature on iPhone and Android, and it scrolls while you record portrait and landscape in the same take), or a teleprompter app on a second device. You know within two seconds when someone's reading. The eyes track left to right, the delivery flattens, and every sentence lands with the same rhythm. The fix isn't memorising your script. It's using a teleprompter properly, and most people set theirs up wrong.

## The setup problem nobody mentions

Standalone teleprompter apps have a structural flaw: they're not the camera. Your options with them are all bad in different ways. Film through the teleprompter app's built-in camera and you give up your camera app's quality and controls. Run the teleprompter on a second device and you're now reading two feet from the lens, which makes the "reading eyes" problem worse, not better. Prop a printed script next to the phone and your eye-line is off by 20 degrees.

The distance between where your eyes point and where the lens sits is the whole game. Every centimetre of gap is visible in the footage.

## Setup that works

### 1. Put the script on the recording device, near the lens

ContentCam's [Built-in Teleprompter](https://contentcam.app/features/teleprompter/) overlays the script on the camera screen itself, and you can set the reading line to position 1, the highest, closest to the front camera. Your eyes end up a centimetre or two from the lens instead of a few degrees off it. At normal viewing distance (arm's length or a tripod a metre away), that gap disappears.

### 2. Distance is your friend

The further the camera is from your face, the smaller the angle between lens and text. At 1.5 metres, even a mid-screen reading line looks like eye contact. If your setup allows it, move the phone back and use a bigger text size.

### 3. Match scroll speed to your talking pace, not your reading pace

Everyone sets the scroll too fast. You read silently at maybe 250 words per minute; you speak at 130 to 150. Set the scroll so it feels slightly too slow, then do one test take. If you're waiting on the text, nudge it up. Chasing the text is what produces that breathless, flat delivery.

### 4. One idea per line

Format the script in short lines, one thought each, like this:

> Here's the thing about white balance.
Auto changes its mind mid-take.
Your footage shifts orange, then blue.
And you can't fix it consistently in post.

Short lines give your eyes a single fixation point per thought, which reads as natural pauses instead of a continuous scan.

## Write for your mouth, not your eyes

Scripts that read well on paper sound like essays out loud. Before recording:

- Read it aloud once and rewrite every sentence you stumbled on. Stumbles aren't your fault; they're the sentence's fault.
- Use contractions everywhere. "You will notice" becomes "you'll notice".
- Cut sentences over ~15 words in half. You can't breathe mid-sentence on camera without it showing.
- Write the first line word-for-word and nail it. Viewers decide in the first three seconds; that's the one part that can't be loose.

## A rehearsal routine that takes four minutes

1. Read the script aloud once, off-camera. Fix stumbles. (2 min)
2. One throwaway take at your chosen scroll speed. Watch it back only for pace. (1 min)
3. Adjust speed, then record for real. Most scripts land within two real takes. (1 min)

That's it. Four minutes of setup replaces the twenty takes of trying to remember your third bullet point.

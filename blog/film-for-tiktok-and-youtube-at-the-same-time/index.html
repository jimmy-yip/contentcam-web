How to Film for TikTok and YouTube at the Same Time

By Jimmy, developer of ContentCam · 5 July 2026

The awkward truth of multi-platform publishing: your best-performing content needs to exist in two shapes that share almost no pixels. Here's the format reality, then how to shoot one take that works in both.

What each platform actually wants

PlatformFormatResolutionNotes
TikTok9:16 vertical1080×1920UI covers the right edge (buttons) and bottom (caption)
Instagram Reels9:16 vertical1080×1920Bottom ~20% covered by caption/audio UI
YouTube Shorts9:16 vertical1080×1920Title and buttons overlay bottom and right
YouTube (main)16:9 horizontal1920×1080 or 3840×2160Clean frame, no overlays, viewers expect wide shots

So it's really two deliverables: one 9:16 file for the three vertical platforms, one 16:9 for YouTube proper. The mistake is treating one as the "real" version and the other as an export setting.

Framing one shot that survives both crops

If you record with a dual-format app, both versions come from the same moment, so the framing decisions happen before you press record, not in the edit.

Keep the subject in the vertical core

Picture a 9:16 column running down the middle of your scene. Anything that must be visible in the vertical version (you, the product, the text you're pointing at) lives in that column. The landscape version then gains the space either side of it: background, environment, second subject.

Respect the overlay zones

On the vertical file, the bottom fifth and the right edge get covered by platform UI. Faces and key action sit in the upper two-thirds, centre-left. ContentCam has a safe-zone overlay you can toggle so this is visible while framing.

Give the landscape a reason to exist

A wide shot of a plain wall isn't a second deliverable, it's the same shot with more wall. Position yourself so the extra width shows something: the workspace, the location, the before/after. On iPhone 11+, Dual Lens mode takes this further by recording the ultra-wide as your landscape, a genuinely different framing from the portrait.

The one-take workflow

  1. Set up once. Tripod or propped phone, both framings checked in the preview (ContentCam shows the landscape tile while you frame the portrait).
  2. Lock what shouldn't drift. If light is mixed, lock white balance; if you move around, lock exposure. Both files inherit the same settings, so they'll match in the edit.
  3. Record the take. One button. Script on the teleprompter if it's a talking piece.
  4. Review both immediately. The side-by-side review shows whether the landscape actually works or you were kidding yourself about that framing. Re-take now, not after packing up.
  5. Edit in parallel. Files arrive named project_scene_001_Portrait.mov and project_scene_001_Landscape.mov. Same take number, same audio, cuts line up 1:1 across both timelines.

What about just cropping in post?

You can, and for tight talking heads it's passable. But a 16:9 crop from a 1080×1920 portrait is a 1080×607 slice upscaled to 1920×1080, roughly 3× the pixel area invented by the upscaler, showing less of the scene than your portrait did. The full breakdown of why that hurts, and the capture methods that avoid it, is in this guide.

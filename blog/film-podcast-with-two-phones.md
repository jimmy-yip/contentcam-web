# How to Film a Podcast With Two Phones (2026)

Source: https://contentcam.app/blog/film-podcast-with-two-phones/

Two phones, two people, one table: where to place each phone, which settings to match, how to sync the files, the audio setup that actually works, and how to stop a phone dying mid-record.

By Jimmy, developer of ContentCam · 24 September 2026

Search for how to record a podcast and every result assumes you and your guest are in different cities, on Riverside recording in the browser, or on a Zoom call. Two people at the same table with a phone each is the setup most people actually have, and almost nobody writes about it. Two phones will give you a real two-camera podcast. What two phones will not do on their own is stay in sync, match each other's colour, or record usable audio.

## Where do you put two phones for a podcast?

Give each person their own phone, at that person's seated eye level, 1.2 to 1.5 metres away, placed just past the opposite shoulder of the other speaker rather than dead centre. Each phone then sees its subject at a slight three-quarter angle, looking across the lens at a person rather than down the barrel at a machine.

Seated eye level means measured while both people are sitting. A tripod left at standing height and tilted down is what makes a podcast look like security footage. The 1.2 to 1.5 metre distance is about the face rather than the framing: a phone's main lens inside a metre stretches the nose and narrows the ears. Use the telephoto lens from that distance if the phone has a 2x or 3x one, since a 5x crops in too tight for a seated two-shot.

Frame each close up with the face, the microphone and any product inside the centre third of the frame. Crop a widescreen 16:9 recording to a full-height 9:16 vertical and about 32% of the original width survives, so anything near the edges is gone from the clips you post to TikTok, Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts. If you own a third phone, put it 2.5 metres back for a wide two shot to carry the cold open and the moments where both people talk at once.

## What settings should you match across both phones?

Match four things before recording: frame rate, resolution, white balance and exposure. All four are set per phone, and two phones left on automatic will disagree about all four inside the first five minutes.

- **Frame rate.** One number on both phones. 30fps is the safe podcast default; 24fps looks more filmic and cuts fine as long as both phones are on 24. Never mix.
- **Resolution.** 1080p on both beats 4K on one and 1080p on the other. Choose 4K on both only if you plan to punch in on the close ups while editing.
- **White balance.** A fixed Kelvin value, the same on each phone. Roughly 5600K when daylight through a window is the main source, roughly 4000K under warm domestic LED bulbs. Automatic white balance drifts whenever someone in a red shirt leans forward, and two phones drift in different directions, which is how one person ends up orange and the other blue in the same cut.
- **Exposure.** Lock it after framing, with the subject already sitting in position. Automatic exposure rides up and down every time a hand crosses the frame.

Two different phone models will still not match exactly. An iPhone and a Pixel render skin tones differently on identical settings, so record 20 seconds of both people and fix the difference before the real recording.

## How do you sync two phones recording a podcast?

Clap once, loudly, with both people in their own frame, immediately after both phones are rolling. The clap puts a sharp spike in both audio tracks and a visible hand position in both video tracks, and Premiere, Resolve, Final Cut and CapCut all line two clips up from that spike in seconds.

Clap again at the end, because two phones run off their own clocks and never tick at exactly the same rate. The difference is small, usually a fraction of a second across an hour, but it is enough that a cut lined up at the start can sit slightly late by the end. A clap at each end tells you whether drift is present and how much. Start both phones before anyone speaks and stop both after everyone stops: overlap is the goal, not simultaneous starts.

## What is the best audio setup for a two phone podcast?

Record the audio on something other than the two cameras. A phone 1.3 metres from a face is a long way for a built-in microphone, which collects the room, the fridge and the air conditioning along with the voice.

In order of how much each option helps:

- **A clip-on microphone on each person.** On the centre line of the chest, roughly 20cm below the chin, cable tucked out of shot. Most wireless clip-on kits ship with two transmitters and one receiver for exactly this.
- **One microphone between you.** A single clip-on on the table, 40 to 60cm from each mouth. Not as clean as two, far better than either phone.
- **A third phone as an audio recorder.** Face down on the table between you, recording a voice memo. Free, and still better than the camera phones because it is closer.

Then fix the room, which is cheaper than any microphone. A carpeted room with a sofa and curtains sounds better than a large tiled one. Turn off the air conditioner and anything with a compressor, and put every phone in Do Not Disturb so vibration alerts do not buzz the table. Record 10 seconds of silence before anyone talks: that clean sample of the room is what noise reduction software needs to work.

## How do you stop a phone from stopping mid-recording?

Three things end a long phone recording early: storage, heat and battery. All three are predictable, so check them before the guest arrives.

Storage is plain arithmetic. Divide the recording bitrate in megabits by 8 to get megabytes per second. A 50 Mbps stream is 6.25 MB every second, 375 MB a minute, 22.5 GB an hour, so a 90 minute episode needs about 34 GB on each phone. Two phones at the same resolution can still write very different file sizes, because HEVC packs the same picture smaller than H.264 does. Read the bitrate your own phone reports for the mode you picked, multiply, then clear that much space.

Heat is the one that surprises people. A phone recording high resolution video for an hour gets hot, and a hot phone slows down, dims its screen or stops recording. Take the case off, keep both phones out of direct sun, and do not stand either one on a laptop or a power brick. Battery drains fast enough that plugging in is worth doing by default, though charging generates heat too, so run the room cooler than feels necessary.

## Common questions

**Can two phones replace two proper cameras for a podcast?** For a talking podcast, yes. A modern phone at 1080p is not the weak link in a conversation video. Audio and lighting are.

**Do both phones need to be the same model?** No, but matching models makes colour matching much easier. If the two phones differ, record a short test of both people and fix the colour difference before the real recording.

**How do you sync two phone recordings without a clapperboard?** Clap once on camera. Premiere, Resolve and Final Cut can also sync by audio waveform, and a clap gives that feature a sharp transient to lock onto.

**Should each phone film vertical or horizontal for a podcast?** Horizontal for the full episode, because YouTube and podcast platforms want 16:9.

How to Record the Front and Back Camera at the Same Time on iPhone

By Jimmy, developer of ContentCam · 11 August 2026

You're watching something and your face does the thing: the eyebrow raise, the "no way." The reaction and the thing that caused it are the whole video, and they're on two different cameras. Film it the usual way and you're either holding a second phone or faking the reaction later. Recording the front and back cameras together gets both in one take: the rear camera on what you're filming, the selfie camera on you, running at the same time.

What it's actually good for

A few formats live or die on having both cameras at once:

  • Reactions. Your face over the clip, video, or message you're reacting to. The timing has to be real, and it only is when both record together.
  • Unboxings and demos. Your hands on the product from the rear camera, your face reacting from the front. One take, no cutting between two clips.
  • POV with commentary. Show the view, the meal, the workout, with you in the corner talking through it as it happens.
  • Tutorials. Point the rear camera at what you're doing while your face explains it, so viewers get the steps and the person at once.

The common thread is that the two shots have to be simultaneous to work. A reaction filmed after the fact reads as fake, because it is.

How front-and-back recording works

Recent iPhones can run more than one camera at the same time. A front-and-back mode uses that: it records your rear lens and your front lens together and puts your face into the frame, usually as a smaller inset over the main shot. You press record once and both cameras roll on the same timeline, so audio and reactions stay in sync with no work in the edit.

This is a different thing from recording two rear cameras for two framings of the same scene. That's dual-lens, and it solves a different problem. Front and back is about you plus what you're seeing.

Shooting it well

The front camera is the weaker sensor and the audio is easy to get wrong, so a few things make a real difference:

  • Light your face. Sit facing a window or a lamp. The selfie camera gets noisy in dim rooms faster than the rear one does, and your face is the part people watch.
  • Mind the inset. Leave a clear corner for your face so it doesn't sit over the thing you're showing. Frame the rear shot with that corner already spoken for.
  • Talk toward the phone. Phone mics favour whoever's closest and loudest. If you're a step back showing something, your voice drops. Keep it up, or come closer for the lines that matter.
  • Lock exposure if you move. Walking from a bright window to a dark hallway makes both cameras hunt. Lock it so the shot doesn't pump. ContentCam's manual controls hold it steady.

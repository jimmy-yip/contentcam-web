How to Film a Reaction Video on Your Phone

By Jimmy, developer of ContentCam · 10 September 2026 · Updated 5 November 2026

To film a reaction video on a phone, record your face and the thing you are reacting to in the same take. If the reaction is going to be edited or posted to more than one platform, record the two cameras as separate files: ContentCam's Front & Back mode does this on iPhone and Android (5 free recordings, then Premium). For a quick one-off post, the free built-in options are Instagram's Dual, Snapchat's Dual Camera and Dual Capture on newer iPhones, and each saves one combined video.

Disclosure: I build ContentCam. The built-in features are described from Apple, Meta and Snapchat's own pages.

How do you film a reaction video on a phone?

There are four routes to recording both in one take.

  • ContentCam's Front & Back mode. The selfie camera and the rear camera record at the same time as two separate files. It works on every iPhone that runs iOS 18, including iPhone 15 and iPhone 16, which do not have Apple's Dual Capture, and on Android phones whose cameras can run together. Afterwards, the Combine editor can render the pair as picture-in-picture or split screen.
  • Dual Capture in the iPhone Camera app. Apple lists seven models: iPhone 17, iPhone 17 Pro, iPhone 17 Pro Max, iPhone Air, iPhone 18 Pro, iPhone 18 Pro Max and iPhone Duo. It saves one video, with the front camera in a window over the rear camera.
  • Instagram's Dual or Snapchat's Dual Camera. Meta describes Dual as a way to "record content and your reaction at the same time". Snapchat's Dual Camera has four layouts, picked before you record, on iPhone XS or later; its help centre still says Android is coming "soon". Both give you one clip inside the app.
  • Screen recording plus your face. If you are reacting to something on the phone itself, screen record and film yourself separately, then stack them in the edit.

Where should the small frame go?

Put your face in a corner, and keep it out of the bottom third.

Every vertical platform layers its own interface over the frame. The bottom of a TikTok or a Reel carries the caption, the username and a row of buttons, and the right edge carries the action column.

Top left is the safest corner. Keep the face frame large enough to read an expression, which in practice means at least a fifth of the frame width.

With Dual Capture or Snapchat's Dual Camera you set this before you press record. With two separate files you place and size the face frame in the edit, and can place it differently for each platform.

How do you fix the audio problem?

You are reacting to something that makes sound, while making sound yourself. If both play out loud into one microphone, they compete, and your phone's automatic level control will duck one of them unpredictably.

Three fixes, in order of how well they work:

  • Wear headphones and add the source audio in the edit. Your microphone hears only you, cleanly, and you lay the original audio underneath afterwards at whatever level you want. This is the real answer.
  • Screen record, which captures the source audio on its own track. Then your voice stays separate by default.
  • If both must play into one mic, put the source speaker well behind you and keep your own mic within about 30cm of your mouth. The distance ratio does the separating.

Whatever you choose, turn off any automatic gain control the app offers. It is what makes reaction audio pump up and down.

How do you make a reaction look natural?

React late rather than early, and do not narrate.

The common failure is performing the reaction before the moment lands, because you already know what is coming. If you have already seen it, react to a detail you missed rather than faking surprise at the main beat.

Leave the silences in. A pause with a changing expression holds attention better than filling the gap with commentary, and it gives you clean cut points.

Why record the two angles as separate files?

Because the built-in effects hand you one combined clip. Apple's Dual Capture, for example, puts the front camera in a window inside the frame, so you cannot move the small frame afterwards, resize it, or cut to one angle on its own.

Separate files fix all three. You can cut to your face for the big moment, run the scene full screen while you stay quiet, and build a vertical and a widescreen layout from the same take. That is why ContentCam's Front & Back saves two files: combine them in its Combine editor, or take both into CapCut or Premiere.

The built-in effects suit one thing well: a quick reaction you will post straight from the app with no editing.

Common questions

Can you film a reaction video with just one phone? Yes. ContentCam's Front & Back records your face and the scene as two files on iPhone and Android. Instagram's Dual, Snapchat's Dual Camera and Dual Capture on seven iPhone models each record one combined video.

What is the best app for filming reaction videos? For a reaction you will edit or post to several platforms, one that saves your face and the scene as separate files, such as ContentCam's Front & Back. For a quick post with no editing, Instagram's Dual or Snapchat's Dual Camera is free.

How do you stop your voice and the video audio fighting? Wear headphones so only your voice reaches the microphone, then add the source audio in the edit.

Where should the face cam go in a vertical video? Top left. The bottom third and the right edge are covered by platform interface.

Why does my reaction video only work on one platform? Because it was captured in one aspect ratio, and a widescreen crop of a vertical frame keeps only about 32% of the height.

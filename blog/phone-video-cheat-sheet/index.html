The Phone Video Cheat Sheet

By Jimmy, developer of ContentCam · 20 September 2026

Most filming advice is written as prose, which is the wrong shape for it. You do not read a paragraph while you are standing in a cafe deciding which lens to be on. You want a table you can glance at.

So this is that. Four tables and a checklist, no story around them. Everything here is the stuff I actually reach for, and it is all technique rather than gear, so none of it expires when a new phone comes out.

Which lens and frame rate, by situation

The single most common reason footage looks cheap is being on the wrong lens. The ultrawide has the smallest sensor on the phone and bends everything near the edges, which is unflattering on a face and unforgiving in low light. If you are unsure, you are on 1x.

SituationWhat to use
Filming yourself talking1x · 30fps · lock exposure
A room or a whole space0.5x · 30fps · hold still
Anything you will slow down1x · 60fps · bright light only
Indoors under ceiling lights1x · 25fps if you see banding
Hands, food, close detail1x · step closer, do not zoom
Walking with the phone1x · elbows in · 30fps
Golden hour outside1x · exposure down a notch
A dark room or a bar1x only · never 0.5x
Something far awayThe telephoto lens, not pinching
Posting to more than one platform4K 30, then crop for each

On frame rate: 30 is the safe default everywhere. 24 gives you slightly more motion blur between frames, which is the look people mean when they say cinematic, but it also means you have to pan more slowly. 60 is for footage you intend to slow down later. Played at normal speed it reads like a home camcorder, which is the opposite of what most people expect when they turn it on.

The banding one catches people out. If you film indoors and see faint stripes rolling up through the frame, that is your lights flickering at mains frequency and your shutter not matching it. Switching between 25 and 30fps usually kills it.

Where to stand, in any light

Light position does more for a shot than any setting, and it costs nothing. Straight-on light flattens a face because it removes every shadow that gives it shape. Move so the window is off to one side and the shape comes back.

The lightWhere you go
A big window, daytimeSide on to it, about a metre back
Harsh midday sunFind shade, or put it behind you
Golden hour outsideFace into it, exposure down
Overcast, flat greyGet closer to anything bright
One lamp in a dark roomLamp beside you, never behind
Overhead ceiling lightsTurn them off, use a window
A cafe with mixed lightFace the window, not the room
Street lights at nightUnder one, not between two
A white wall nearbyFace it. It bounces light back at you
No good light at allFilm your hands, not your face

That last row is not a joke. A hands shot in bad light is usable. A face in bad light is not, and no amount of grading afterwards will fix it.

What each setting costs you when it is wrong

Every one of these is a switch you can find in under a minute, and every one of them quietly degrades everything you shoot until you change it.

SettingWhat it costs you
HDR video onLooks right on your phone, unpredictable once a platform re-encodes it
High-efficiency formatSmaller files, and apps that refuse to open them without converting
60fps by defaultReads like a home camcorder at normal speed
Beauty filter onSkin texture removed at capture. It does not come back
Auto exposureThe shot brightens and darkens every time you turn
View outside the frameYou spend the take watching the part you are not recording
0.5x indoorsSoft, noisy, and bent at the edges
Capturing at 1080No crop room and no room to stabilise afterwards
Text in the bottom quarterCovered by the caption and the handle
The front cameraSmaller sensor and a softer lens than the back

The capture-resolution one is worth spelling out, because it gets stated backwards constantly. Filming in 4K does not mean your post is delivered in 4K, and on most platforms it is not. What the extra pixels buy you is room: room to crop in, room to reframe, room for software stabilisation to shave the edges off without softening what is left. Shoot big, finish small.

What you see, and what to do about it

This is the one to keep. It is indexed by the symptom, which is how you actually arrive at the problem: you look at a clip, something is wrong, and you do not have the vocabulary to name it.

What you seeWhat to do
Your face dark, the window blown outTap and hold on your face to lock exposure there
Everything looks flatMove so the light is off to one side
Harsh shadows under the eyesThe light is too high. Get lower or diffuse it
Orange face, blue backgroundTwo light sources of different colour. Pick one
Grainy and soft indoorsMore light, or drop back to 1x
The shot brightens as you turnLock exposure before you move
Stripes rolling through the frameSwitch between 25 and 30fps
Blown-out white skyDrag the exposure slider down
Skin looks plasticA beauty filter is on. Turn it off
Washed out after postingHDR video is on. Turn it off

The checklist, before you record

Nine things, about forty seconds. This is the part that actually saves footage, because every item on it is something you cannot fix afterwards.

  • Lens on 1x, not 0.5x
  • Tap and hold until focus and exposure lock
  • Exposure dragged down a touch
  • Grid on, horizon level
  • Nothing important in the bottom quarter of the frame
  • The light is off to one side of you
  • Something close to the lens, for depth
  • Two seconds of handles at each end of the take
  • The background is still, not busy

The handles one is the cheapest habit on the list. Start recording two seconds before anything happens and stop two seconds after it finishes. Almost every clip that has to be binned is missing those four seconds.

And before you post

  • Exported once, not twice
  • Text clear of the caption band
  • The first frame works as a cover on its own
  • Sound checked on speakers, not headphones
  • It still looks right on a screen that did not film it
  • You held the last shot one beat longer than felt right
  • The file came from your edit, not from downloading your own post

That last one matters more than it sounds. Saving your own video back off a platform and re-uploading it somewhere else puts the footage through a second round of compression, on top of a file that was already compressed once on the way up. Always go back to the export.

The one thing not on any of these tables

Every table above assumes you are shooting one framing and living with it. If you post to more than one place, you are either cropping a vertical video into a horizontal one, which never looks right because the composition was not built for it, or you are filming the same thing twice.

That is the problem ContentCam exists for: it records vertical and horizontal at the same time, from one take, so the wide shot is a real wide shot rather than a crop. There is more on how that works here, and on why cropping between the two rarely survives.
